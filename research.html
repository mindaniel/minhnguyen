Is Biodiversity Risk Priced in Mutual Funds?

Does the firm-level biodiversity risk premium survive aggregation into mutual fund returns, or do diversification and fees dilute the signal?

← Back to profile Request the draft
Abstract

Abstract

This paper investigates whether the firm-level biodiversity risk premium is transmitted into mutual fund returns, or if aggregation, diversification, and fund fees dilute the risk signal. Building on the equilibrium framework of Pástor et al. (2021), we analyse an unbalanced panel of 10,366 active equity mutual funds from December 2006 to February 2025 using LSEG data. Fund-level biodiversity exposure is captured via a novel BioScore that maps industry-specific nature dependencies (ENCORE) against regional ecological fragility (WRI Aqueduct) under a double-materiality framework.

Using decile portfolio sorts and Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional regressions, we find that the biodiversity risk premium is highly dependent on the regional regulatory environment. In Europe, where coordinated frameworks (GBF, SFDR) provide clear pricing signals, high-biodiversity-risk funds generate significant risk-adjusted returns that compensate investors for nature-negative exposure. Conversely, in the United States, where environmental regulation remains fragmented, the premium is not statistically significant.

This study provides the first empirical evidence of how biodiversity risk survives mutual fund aggregation, offering insights for fiduciaries, asset managers, and regulators navigating nature-related financial disclosures.

Method

Empirical strategy

1. Constructing the BioScore

Fund-level biodiversity exposure is built bottom-up from holdings. For fund \(i\) at time \(t\), the score is the value-weighted average of holding-level biodiversity intensity, where each holding's intensity is the product of its industry's nature dependency (ENCORE) and the ecological fragility of the region in which it operates (WRI Aqueduct):

\[ \mathrm{BioScore}_{i,t} = \sum_{j=1}^{N_{i,t}} w_{j,i,t} \left( D_{s(j)} \times F_{r(j)} \right) \] \(w_{j,i,t}\) is the portfolio weight of holding \(j\); \(D_{s(j)}\) the ENCORE dependency score of sector \(s(j)\); \(F_{r(j)}\) the Aqueduct fragility score of region \(r(j)\); \(N_{i,t}\) the number of holdings.

2. Portfolio sorts

Funds are sorted into deciles on \(\mathrm{BioScore}_{i,t-1}\) and rebalanced monthly. The spread portfolio is long the top decile and short the bottom decile, and its risk-adjusted return is the intercept of a Fama–French five-factor plus momentum regression:

\[ R^{\,\mathrm{H\!-\!L}}_{t} = \alpha + \beta_{\mathrm{MKT}}\,\mathrm{MKT}_t + \beta_{\mathrm{SMB}}\,\mathrm{SMB}_t + \beta_{\mathrm{HML}}\,\mathrm{HML}_t + \beta_{\mathrm{RMW}}\,\mathrm{RMW}_t + \beta_{\mathrm{CMA}}\,\mathrm{CMA}_t + \beta_{\mathrm{MOM}}\,\mathrm{MOM}_t + \varepsilon_t \] A significant \(\alpha\) indicates a biodiversity risk premium not explained by conventional factor exposures.

3. Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional regressions

In the first stage, a cross-sectional regression is estimated in each month \(t\); in the second stage, the time series of the resulting coefficients is averaged:

\[ R_{i,t+1} = \gamma_{0,t} + \gamma_{1,t}\,\mathrm{BioScore}_{i,t} + \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2,t}^{\prime}\, \mathbf{X}_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t+1}, \qquad \hat{\gamma}_1 = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \hat{\gamma}_{1,t} \] \(\mathbf{X}_{i,t}\) collects fund controls (size, age, expense ratio, turnover, flow, factor loadings). Standard errors are Newey–West adjusted for autocorrelation.

4. Regional split

The panel is partitioned by the regulatory environment of the fund's domicile. Europe operates under coordinated frameworks — the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF) and the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR) — while the United States remains fragmented. Comparing \(\hat{\gamma}_1\) across the two subsamples isolates the role of regulation in generating a pricing signal.

Findings in brief

  • Europe: high-biodiversity-risk funds earn significant risk-adjusted returns, compensating investors for nature-negative exposure.
  • United States: the premium is not statistically significant under fragmented environmental regulation.
  • Implication: regulatory coordination, not exposure alone, is what turns biodiversity risk into a priced factor.
